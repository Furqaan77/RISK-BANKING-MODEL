01

Why large banks need it

Big banks already have credit scores. The problem is what those scores miss.

Good people turned awayGig workers, shift workers, graduates and newcomers often have thin credit files. Old-style scores treat "little history" as "high risk".
Risk without opportunityCommittees see how much they might lose, but rarely how many good customers and how much profit a policy gives up.
Shocks move faster than reviewsWar, natural disasters, recession and redundancy hit customers months before a missed payment shows.

And the data cannot leave the bank. So the whole system runs on the bank's own servers.

02

Two markets, one platform

The same engine runs in New York and in London, but each place has its own laws. Each country gets its own rule pack, and its own separate installation: American data stays in America, British data stays in Britain.

New York (United States)

  • If the bank says no, or offers less, it must send a letter with the main reasons (up to four), usually within 30 days.
  • For credit cards, the bank must check the customer can afford at least the minimum payment.
  • Under-21s need their own income or an adult co-signer for a card.
  • Interest-rate caps apply, for example for service members (36%) and under New York law.
  • Banned factors such as race, religion or sex can never be used. People aged 62+ can never be scored down for age.

London (United Kingdom)

  • Affordability is its own legal test, separate from risk. A good score cannot make an unaffordable loan acceptable.
  • Income must be checked, not just declared.
  • Under the Consumer Duty, the bank must show good outcomes for every group of customers, including vulnerable people.
  • If a computer decides, the customer can ask for a person to review it and can challenge the decision.
  • Mortgages are tested as if rates rise at least 1 percentage point over five years.
03

How it fits into a big bank

Large banks already have decision systems. AI Risk Score Card Global starts beside them and earns trust step by step.

STEP 1 · CHALLENGERWatch and compareRuns in the shadow on the bank's past and live applications. Decides nothing. Proves it beats the bank's current model.
STEP 2 · ADD-ONFeed a better scoreThe bank's own decision system calls it for a score, affordability check and reasons, in under a third of a second.
STEP 3 · FULL ENGINERun the decisionWhere the bank chooses, it runs the whole decision and the approval queues.

Where can it decide automatically?

Only where the bank switches it on. In our demo: small starter cards and salary advances up to $/£1,000 for low-risk grades, and limit increases for good accounts after a fresh affordability check.

Where is a person always involved?

Every decline. Every mortgage. Every business loan. Anything above the bank's automation line.

The grade scale: from very safe to very risky

Big banks use a "master scale" with many grades. Each grade stands for a range of chances that a customer will fall seriously behind within a year. The system uses the bank's own scale. This demo uses 20 grades.

Grades 1–12: small starter products may be approved automaticallyGrades 13–20: a person always reviews

Each box shows the upper limit of its range. Tom is in grade 12 (up to 2.4%). Maria is in grade 13 (2.4–3.4%). Out of 100 people like Maria, about 3 may fall seriously behind within a year.

Customer segments by income

A bank does not treat a part-time worker and a senior executive the same way. Their needs are different, the risks are different, and a fair limit for one would be unsafe or useless for the other. So the system first places every applicant in an income segment, then applies that segment's rules on top of the law and the bank's own policy. Illustrative demo policy

How the segment is decided, step by step

  1. Verified income only. Gross yearly income from payroll checks, bank-account data (Open Banking), tax data or credit-bureau income tools. A customer simply telling us their salary never sets the segment on its own.
  2. Irregular income is smoothed. Shift, gig, seasonal or self-employed income is averaged over 12 months of real bank credits, with a small penalty if it swings a lot.
  3. Joint applicants are combined. Two applicants on one loan add their verified incomes together.
  4. Checked again every night. A customer moves up after 2 months at the higher income and down only after 3 months at the lower income, so one bad month doesn't change their segment. A verified job loss moves them down at once.
  5. Then the segment's rules apply, always after the law (US or UK rules) and the bank's own policy. The final limit is still the lowest of all the caps.

Keeping segments fair

  • Income is not a protected characteristic, but income bands can act as a stand-in for race, sex, age or disability. So approval rates, limits, prices and decline reasons are monitored by segment, and any big difference must be justified.
  • No customer is declined just for their income, except that S0 customers can't get unsecured products yet. S0 always gets a path forward: a secured card or a credit-builder loan.
  • The model scores behaviour and affordability. A low earner who manages money well can reach a very good grade.
  • Offers and marketing follow segment rules, affordability comes first, and credit is never promoted to anyone under 18.

What happens after approval

Approval is not the end. From the next night onwards, every customer is re-checked automatically. Here is what happens to Maria's card over her first year, step by step.

  1. Every nightRe-score. The whole book (up to 50 million accounts at a large bank) is re-scored overnight with the latest transactions and payments. Each customer gets two scores: the steady one used for decisions, and a "today" score adjusted for the economy.
  2. Every nightLook for early-warning signals. Income shocks (salary stops or drops), credit stress (card use climbing, minimum payments only, new buy-now-pay-later debts), spending warnings (gambling, payday lenders, spending above income) and outside events (a flood near the home, the employer hit by sanctions).
  3. When signals add upWatch-list. A customer joins the watch-list if their grade worsens by 2 or more notches, or if two or more warning signals appear in the same month (demo rule; the bank sets its own).
  4. Next working dayThe team acts. The portfolio team reviews the watch-list. Typical actions: pause limit increases, offer a payment plan or budgeting help, or route to the collections team early, before any payment is missed. A person always decides.
  5. Month 6 and 12Limit review. For starter cards with on-time payments, a limit increase is considered, always with a fresh affordability (UK) or ability-to-pay (US) check.
  6. MonthlyModel health check. Are today's customers still like the ones the model learned from? If the model drifts, a new version is trained and needs approval. If quality drops sharply, the model switches itself off and the bank's previous model takes over.

Mini example: Maria, month 7 Illustrative

Her delivery app income stops for 5 weeks (a signal: income shock). Her card use jumps from 20% to 85% of the limit (a second signal: credit stress). Two signals in one month put her on the watch-list, and her daily score moves from grade 13 to grade 15. The next morning a portfolio analyst sees the alert, pauses her planned 6-month limit increase, and the bank sends a message offering budgeting help and a short payment plan if needed. In month 9 her delivery income is back, card use falls, and she drops off the watch-list.

A day in the life: who does what in the tool

Five roles you will meet in a risk department, and what a normal day looks like for each of them in AI Risk Score Card Global.

Retail credit team

Underwriter

  1. Opens the review queue: cases the system could not approve on its own (like Maria).
  2. For each case, reads the recommendation, the grade, the reasons and the affordability or ability-to-pay sums.
  3. Checks anything unusual in the data panel, such as an income dip or a recent new loan.
  4. Approves, changes the limit, or refers upwards. Any change needs a written reason.
  5. Signs off letter content the bank will send, and watches the 30-day deadline on US cases.
Risk strategy & policy

Strategy analyst

  1. Checks the risk-versus-opportunity frontier: is our policy on the curve or below it?
  2. Looks at opportunity-loss segments, for example gig workers we decline but who repay well.
  3. Tests a policy change in the simulator and runs the fairness tests on it.
  4. Submits the change as a request; a second person (the checker) must approve it.
  5. Writes up the impact for the credit committee.
Model risk & validation

Model validator

  1. Reviews the overnight model health report: accuracy, stability and drift.
  2. Compares the bank's current model with ours on the same customers (champion vs challenger).
  3. Runs fairness tests: US adverse impact ratio and fairer-model search; UK Equality Act tests.
  4. Replays a sample of past decisions to confirm they reproduce exactly.
  5. Approves or rejects a new model version. Nothing goes live without this sign-off.
Collections & recoveries

Collections agent

  1. Opens the list of customers behind on payments, ranked by who is likely to recover and who needs help.
  2. Reads the suggested help option, for example a payment plan or a pause on interest.
  3. Checks for vulnerability flags first; those go to a specialist.
  4. Reviews AI-drafted messages for tone and accuracy before anything is sent.
  5. Respects contact limits: how often, at what times and by which channel.
Leadership

Chief Risk Officer (CRO)

  1. Reads the board dashboard: losses, approvals, opportunity captured, fairness.
  2. Checks the scenarios: what a recession, a war or a flood would do to losses.
  3. Reviews the watch-list trend and climate exposure of mortgaged homes.
  4. Decides on policy changes the strategy team proposes, within the bank's risk appetite.
  5. Reports to the board and to regulators, using the system's evidence packs.

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question in your head, then open it to check.

1. Maria's risk is fine and she can afford $5,000. Why does she get only $1,500?

Because the limit is the lowest of all the caps. The bank's starter-card policy caps thin-file customers at $1,500, which is lower than her request and her budget.

2. Why was Tom approved automatically but Maria was not?

The bank allows automatic approval only for starter products up to $/£1,000 at grade 12 or better. Tom: £1,000, grade 12. Maria: $1,500 and grade 13, so a person reviewed it.

3. In the UK, a customer has an excellent risk grade but fails the affordability test. What happens?

They cannot be approved as asked. A good score never overrides failed affordability. The case goes to a person, who may offer a smaller amount or help instead.

4. Who chooses the reasons in a US adverse-action letter: the AI writer or the rules?

The rules and the model's reason codes. The AI writer may only turn them into plain words, and a person approves the letter.

5. What is expected loss, and what was it for Tom?

The average yearly cost of the risk: PD × LGD × EAD. For Tom: 2.2% × 75% × £750 ≈ £12 a year.

6. Can the system decline an application on its own?

No. Automation can only approve, and only where the bank allows it. Every decline is reviewed by a person.

7. A 16-year-old has a junior account. What may the bank do?

Record good money habits for a readiness record and offer non-credit loyalty such as savings boosts. No credit, no credit marketing, and no marketing profiling until 18.

8. Two warning signals appear for the same customer in one month. What happens next?

The customer joins the watch-list. The next working day a person reviews it and may pause limit increases or offer help before any payment is missed.

9. Maria earns about $48,000 a year. Which income segment is she in, and why is her card limit not $5,000?

She is in S2 Core / blue-collar ($30,000–59,999). S2 allows cards of $1,500–5,000, but she also has the thin-file tag, and the thin-file starter cap of $1,500 is lower. The limit is always the lowest of all the caps.

10. A customer's income drops for one month. Do they move down a segment straight away?

No. Customers move down only after 3 months at the lower income (or at once if a job loss is verified), and up after 2 months at a higher income. One bad month doesn't change the rules they are judged by.

How it gets built

Four releases are built by parallel teams. The product owner's target is everything within about 3 months with 14–18 people. That is ambitious and marked high-risk, so the releases are prioritised: if time runs short, the earliest releases are already complete.

Build (target, high risk)Pilot at a bank

What each release brings

R1 Challenger MVP: cards, loans and salary advance in both countries; champion-vs-challenger report; first frontier; demo US and UK banks.

R2 Pilot-ready: mortgages, small business and corporate, connections to the bank's systems, letters, logins and approval queues.

R3 Growth & macro: inclusion engine, Gen Z, product designer, offers, crisis scenarios, fairness search, validator in a box.

R4 Operations: collections help, UK persistent-debt rules, car loans, overdrafts, buy-now-pay-later, document reading, policy copilot.

The pilot path

1. Back-test on 3–5 years of the bank's history, including people it turned down.

2. Six months in the shadow next to the bank's current model.

3. Live on one product, with people reviewing.

A large bank's own approval of a new supplier usually takes 12–24 months. That runs in parallel and does not get faster just because the build is fast.

Words used on this page

PD (probability of default)
The chance a customer falls 90 or more days behind within the next 12 months. Maria: 3.1%. Tom: 2.2%.
LGD (loss given default)
If a customer does default, the share of the money owed that the bank expects to lose. 75% for cards in our examples.
EAD (exposure at default)
How much the customer is likely to owe at the moment they default. For a new card, part of the limit.
Expected loss
The average yearly cost of the risk: PD × LGD × EAD. Compared with what the product earns.
Grade / master scale
The bank's list of risk grades from safest to riskiest. Each grade is a band of PD. Our demo uses 20 grades.
Champion
The bank's current credit model.
Challenger
A new model tested next to the champion on the same customers, to see which does better.
Affordability
Whether someone can repay without hardship. In the UK it is a separate legal test from credit risk (CONC 5.2A).
Ability-to-pay
The US rule that a card issuer must check the customer can afford at least the minimum payment (CARD Act).
DTI / DBR
Debt-to-income or debt-burden ratio: the share of monthly income that goes on debt repayments.
Thin file
A credit record with very little history, common for young people, newcomers and gig workers.
Credit bureau / credit reference agency
A company (such as Equifax, Experian or TransUnion) that keeps records of how people have repaid credit.
Open Banking
Sharing your bank-account data with your consent, used to check income and spending.
Reason code
A fixed, numbered reason (for example "limited credit history") produced by the rules and model, never invented by AI.
Adverse action notice
The US letter a bank must send when it declines or offers less, with the specific main reasons, usually within 30 days.
Article 22C rights
UK rights when a computer makes a significant decision: to be told, to give your view, to ask for a person and to challenge it.
Consumer Duty
The UK rule that banks must deliver good outcomes for retail customers, including vulnerable people, and prove it.
Automation rule
A rule the bank sets that says which cases the system may approve without a person. It never covers declines.
Maker-checker
One person proposes a change, a different person approves it. Used for policies, models and limits.
Back-test
Running a model on past applications to see how it would have performed, using what actually happened.
Shadow run
Running the new model next to the old one on live applications without letting it decide, to compare results.
Drift
When today's customers start to look different from the ones a model learned from, so it may become less accurate.
Frontier
The best possible balance of profit and losses across lending policies. Policies below it give up profit or customers needlessly.
Stress scenario
A "what if" story, such as a recession or a flood, used to see how losses would change.
Fairness tests (AIR, LDA)
Checks that a model doesn't treat protected groups unfairly. AIR compares approval rates between groups; LDA is a search for an equally accurate but fairer model.
Tokenisation
Replacing names and ID numbers with codes, so personal data is protected even inside the bank.
Income segment
A band of verified yearly income (S0 to S5) that decides which products, limits and checks apply. Maria and Tom are both in S2.
Overlay tag
An extra label such as Gen Z, thin file or gig worker. It changes how a customer is checked, not which income segment they are in.
Residual income
The money left each month after tax, existing debts, the new repayment and essential living costs. Each segment has a minimum.
Watch-list
Customers showing early warning signs, reviewed by people before any payment is missed.
